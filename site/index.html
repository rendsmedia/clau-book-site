About the book

Roads start on someone's land

Too many road projects in Uganda begin before the land is ready. This book follows a project from first planning to the year after the road opens, and shows where land acquisition goes wrong and how to get it right.

For government

Ministries, departments and authorities

A phased process that has land ready before contractors arrive, saving time and public money.

For practitioners

Surveyors, valuers, lawyers, sociologists

The working detail: strip maps, gazetting, valuation, disclosure, titling and records.

For communities

Families whose land is affected

What the law entitles you to, what happens at each stage, and how to protect your household.

Key questions

Building roads while protecting landowners

Every road project has to balance public need with private rights. These are seven of the questions the book works through, with lessons from Uganda's own reforms and from its neighbours.

Chapter 1 · Chapter 8

When should compensation be paid: before or after the land is taken?

Uganda's Constitution requires prompt, fair and adequate compensation before government takes possession, a strong protection for owners.

Kenya and Tanzania allow possession first, with interest paid until compensation is settled. The book compares the two approaches and asks what Uganda could adapt to deliver roads sooner while keeping owners protected.

Chapters 3–5

How do you earn a community's trust?

Early meetings, elected grievance committees and clear disclosure turn affected families into partners in the project rather than opponents of it.

Engage before the first survey peg.
Chapter 1 · 8

How can valuations be agreed sooner?

Clear national valuation standards and a firm legal footing for the valuer's office would reduce disputes. The proposed Valuation Bill points the way.

A reform already under way.
Chapter 1

How should compensation be shared on kibanja land?

Kibanja holders and registered owners often share compensation by common practice. The book makes the case for clear, written rules everyone can rely on.

Clarity helps owners and agencies alike.
Chapter 4

What happens after a corridor is gazetted?

Gazetting sets a cut-off date and pauses new developments. The book explains how to match it to funding and timelines so families aren't left waiting.

Plan the money with the map.
Chapter 8

Why a dedicated budget for land works

Since FY 2018/19, land acquisition at UNRA has had its own budget line. The result: faster payments, fewer contractor claims and fewer court cases.

A reform that paid off.
Chapter 5

How can families get the papers they need?

Letters of administration and powers of attorney can delay a payment. The book sets out how project teams can help, especially vulnerable households.

Support reaches more rightful owners.

The full discussion, with cases and recommendations, is in the book.

The author

William Matovu

A land surveyor and land acquisition, resettlement and social sustainability specialist. He was Head of Land Acquisition at the Uganda National Roads Authority for five years until December 2020, and earlier headed land surveying during the transformation of Kampala City Council into KCCA.

He is a fellow of the Institute of Surveyors of Uganda, holds a BSc in Land Surveying and an MSc in Management, and trains trainers in financial literacy.
