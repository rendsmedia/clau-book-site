About the book

Roads start on someone's land

Too many road projects in Uganda begin before the land is ready. This book follows a project from first planning to the year after the road opens, and shows where land acquisition goes wrong and how to get it right.

For government

Ministries, departments and authorities

A phased process that has land ready before contractors arrive, saving time and public money.

For practitioners

Surveyors, valuers, lawyers, sociologists

The working detail: strip maps, gazetting, valuation, disclosure, titling and records.

For communities

Families whose land is affected

What the law entitles you to, what happens at each stage, and how to protect your household.

The debates

Is it building Uganda, or legal land grabbing?

Seven questions the book takes on. Some will make officials uncomfortable; some will make landowners angry. All come straight from its pages.

Chapter 1 · Chapter 8

Should government take your land before paying you?

Uganda's Constitution says no: payment must be prompt, fair, adequate and made first. The Supreme Court struck down part of the Land Acquisition Act for allowing otherwise.

Kenya and Tanzania let the state take possession first and pay interest while it owes. The author argues Uganda should look hard at that model, because the cost of waiting falls on every taxpayer.

Chapter 6

Is compulsory acquisition just "legal" land grabbing?

Many affected families see it that way. The book argues the power isn't the problem; late planning and late payment are.

Even with timely payment, some dissatisfaction persists.
Chapter 1 · 8

Who gave the Chief Government Valuer the final word?

No law establishes the office, so its valuations are routinely challenged. Tanzania's Chief Valuer, by contrast, is created by statute.

Valuation Bill 2018: still waiting.

Kibanja 70%, landlord 30%. Says who?

That split is how compensation is commonly shared between a kibanja holder and the registered owner. No law or regulation sets it.

Practice, not law.

For affected families

A road is coming through your land. What now?

The five moments that matter most to a household, in the order they happen.

  1. Go to the meetings

    Before any survey, the project meets the community. A grievance committee is elected from your village to carry complaints.

  2. Stop building at the cut-off

    Once the corridor is gazetted, new buildings or crops are not eligible for compensation.

  3. Check the strip map

    It's displayed publicly. Confirm your name and plot boundaries before the valuation is final.

  4. Have your papers ready

    Title, sale agreement, letters of administration. Missing documents are a top cause of delay.

  5. Plan the money first

    A 15% disturbance allowance is added, or 30% with under six months' notice. Replace land, house and income first.

General information from the book, not legal advice.
